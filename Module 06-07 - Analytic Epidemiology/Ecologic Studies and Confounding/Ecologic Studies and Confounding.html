Ecologic Studies and Confounding

APHS 20203 · Epidemiology & Biostatistics

Hierarchy of Study Designs

  • Here is a review of our study design hierarchy.

Ecologic Studies

  • Ecologic studies compare groups, such as cities, schools, or countries.

  • Researchers analyze group characteristics, such as average income and average blood pressure.

  • They can describe population patterns and investigate questions about policies or environments.

  • A relationship between groups does not necessarily describe the relationship between individuals.

  • The important distinction is the level of the question and analysis.

  • Ecologic studies can address population questions directly, as well as generate hypotheses for individual-level research.

  • The hierarchy on the previous slide is a broad guide to individual causal inference, not a ranking of how well each design answers every possible question.

Unit of Observation vs. Unit of Analysis

Hypothetical example: Measure residents’ income and blood pressure, then compare city averages.

Term What Does It Mean? In This Example
Unit of observation The entity we collect measurements from Each resident
Unit of analysis The entity we compare and draw conclusions about Each city

The same individual measurements could support a different analysis that compares residents instead of cities.

  • Definitions adapted from DeCarlo (2018).

  • Here the original observations are residents; aggregating their measurements changes the unit of analysis to cities.

  • If researchers receive only a table of city averages, their available observations are already at the city level.

  • Observation and analysis can be at the same level or different levels.

  • Do not teach that every ecologic study collects individual records.

Ecologic Fallacy

Ecologic fallacy: Assuming a relationship between groups also holds for individuals.

City averages alone do not reveal the relationships within cities.

  • These are invented teaching data, not evidence about income and blood pressure.

  • Each city contains five equally weighted observations. The left panel contains their exact means; the right panel shows the individual observations that produced them.

  • Income is the resident’s reported annual household income, and the blood-pressure measure is systolic blood pressure.

  • Ask students to describe each panel before explaining the error.

  • Between cities, higher average income accompanies higher average blood pressure. Within each city, higher-income residents have lower blood pressure in this constructed example. Neither pattern by itself establishes causation.

  • A reversal makes the distinction easy to see, but ecological fallacy does not require a reversal. The error is assuming an individual association from aggregate data alone, whether the unknown individual association is weaker, absent, reversed, or similar.

Concept: DeCarlo (2018). Figure generated from the hypothetical values in assets/ecologic-fallacy.R.

Practice: What Can We Conclude?

Suppose we have only the city averages from the previous slide. Which conclusion is supported?

A. Increasing a person’s income raises their blood pressure.

B. Within each city, richer residents have higher blood pressure.

C. Cities with higher average income have higher average blood pressure.

D. Income has no relationship with anyone’s blood pressure.

Answer: C. It stays at the level measured in the aggregate data. A adds a causal claim; B assumes an individual association; D also makes an unsupported individual claim. Ask students what information they would need to investigate B. Use a show of hands or letter cards if no live polling tool is configured.

When the Population Is the Question

Hypothetical question: After cities adopt a smoke-free indoor-air policy, do their heart-attack admission rates decline compared with cities that do not?

  • Exposure: A city policy that changes the shared environment.

  • Outcome: The city’s admission rate over time.

  • Why study groups? We want to evaluate the policy’s overall population effect, including effects on people who do not smoke.

  • The analysis must still consider other changes occurring in those cities.

  • So, why would we use an ecological study design?

  • This is a proposed study question, not a claim about a particular policy evaluation.

  • Comparing population trends before and after adoption, with suitable comparison cities, fits the policy question.

  • Individual smoking histories could add information, but they would not replace the question about changing a city’s policy.

  • A simple comparison of cities would not establish causality.

  • Ask what else might change at the same time, such as healthcare access or other tobacco policies.

Dietary Fat and Breast Cancer Mortality

The question: Could differences in diet help explain why breast cancer death rates differ across countries?

Sasaki et al. (1993)

  • Design: Ecologic study of 30 countries, using data from 1961 to 1986.

  • Exposure: National food-supply estimates per person, separated by fat type.

  • Outcome: Female breast cancer mortality rates.

  • Finding: Animal fat excluding fish fat was positively associated with mortality among women aged 50 and older. Fish fat showed inverse associations when statistically significant.

The country averages do not tell us what the women who died had eaten.

  • Start the story with a researcher looking across countries and wondering whether diet contributes to their different death rates.

  • Existing food-balance and mortality records allow a broad comparison without following individual diets for decades.

  • Distinguish national food supply from measured personal consumption.

  • This is an example of a research question, not a claim that this paper initiated the later trial.

  • What is the potential ecological fallacy we have to be careful to avoid?

Low-Fat Dietary Pattern: Randomized Trial

A related question: Does assignment to a dietary program reduce new breast cancers?

Prentice et al. (2006)

  • Participants: 48,835 postmenopausal women, ages 50–79, without prior breast cancer.

  • Intervention: A program to reduce total fat and increase fruit, vegetables, and grains; comparison participants were not asked to change their diet.

  • Outcome: New invasive breast cancers during an average 8.1 years of follow-up.

  • 2006 result: The intervention did not produce a statistically significant reduction in invasive breast cancer incidence.

  • Random assignment strengthens causal inference about assignment to this dietary program.

  • It does not isolate one fat type.

  • A nonsignificant result does not prove the effect is zero.

  • The trial’s reported hazard ratio was 0.91 (95% CI 0.83–1.01); interpreting hazard ratios is not required here. This slide summarizes the cited 2006 report, not subsequent follow-up publications.

Ecologic Studies: Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

  • Fit questions about population policies and environments
  • Reveal patterns across places or time
  • Can use existing data efficiently

Limitations

  • Group associations may not describe individuals
  • Averages can hide differences within groups
  • Other population differences can complicate interpretation

Choose the design that fits the question and the level of the conclusion.

  • Return to the policy example: an ecologic design can be appropriate because the question concerns a population intervention, even when individual data are available.

  • In the dietary study, national food supply is an imperfect proxy for consumption.

  • Ecologic analyses can account for measured population characteristics, but aggregate data do not automatically resolve confounding or differences in measurement between places.

Group Discussion: Comparing Study Designs

Work in groups of 2–3 for 4 minutes. Prepare a brief explanation for each question.

Ecologic Study Randomized Trial
Unit of analysis Countries Individual women
Exposure / intervention National supply of different fats Assignment to a dietary program
Outcome Breast cancer mortality Invasive breast cancer incidence
  1. Why does the ecologic study not tell us whether changing one woman’s diet would change her risk?
  2. What does random assignment add, and what dietary question does this trial actually test?
  3. Why would it be misleading to say that this trial simply proves or disproves the ecologic study?

Purpose: practice matching conclusions to the question, unit of analysis, and design. This is not a competition to name the universally best design.

Facilitation: allow four minutes, then invite a different group to answer each question. Keep the table visible.

Answer key:

  1. The country records do not link each woman’s own exposure to her outcome. Other national differences could contribute to the pattern. Access to effective treatment is one possible influence on mortality; it is a discussion hypothesis, not a finding established by this paper. Mortality involves both developing cancer and surviving it.
  2. Random assignment helps make the groups comparable at baseline. The trial tests assignment to a program with several dietary goals; adherence still matters.
  3. Exposure definitions, populations, and outcomes differ. A stronger design for one causal question does not turn it into a test of every related question. Ask students to name at least two differences from the table.

Sources: Sasaki et al. (1993); Prentice et al. (2006).

From Study Design to Trustworthy Comparisons

The two dietary studies asked different questions. For either study, we still need to ask what could make the comparison misleading.

  • Selection: Who entered the study, and who stayed in it?
  • Information: Were exposure and outcome measured accurately?
  • Confounding: Did the groups differ in other causes of the outcome?

We will connect these questions to the bias examples from the case-control lecture.

This is the transition from choosing a study design to evaluating the comparison it produces.

Social Desirability Bias

  • Definition: Participants report what seems socially acceptable instead of what actually happened.
  • Example: Someone understates their alcohol use because they worry the interviewer will judge them.
  • Problem: The recorded answer differs from the behavior we want to measure.
  • Reducing it: Use neutral questions and protect participants’ privacy.

  • Neutral wording can help, but it does not guarantee accurate reporting.

  • Explain why responses are confidential and consider private self-administered questions when appropriate.

  • Contrast reporting less drinking with actually drinking less because of being observed.

Hawthorne Effect

  • Definition: People change their behavior because they know they are being observed.
  • Example: Participants walk more while a research team monitors their activity.
  • Their recorded activity may be accurate, but it may differ from their usual activity.

Changing behavior and reporting behavior inaccurately are different problems.

  • Observation-related behavior change is not automatically measurement error.

  • Its implications depend on the question: are we interested in usual activity outside a study or activity during a monitored intervention? Preserve this distinction in the exercise.

Practice: Bias and Behavior Changes

For each scenario, identify the bias or behavior change and explain why.

  • Parents of sick children recall pesticide exposure during pregnancy more carefully than parents of healthy children.

  • Participants underreport alcohol use because they worry that the interviewer will judge them.

  • Outcome assessors know who received treatment and rate those participants more favorably.

  • Interviewers ask lung cancer cases more detailed exposure questions than they ask controls.

  • Participants exercise more because they know their activity is being monitored.

Ask for a brief explanation before revealing the next scenario. These mechanisms are stipulated for the exercise; they are not inferred from observed differences alone.

  1. Recall bias: exposure recall differs between groups.
  2. Social desirability bias: concern about judgment changes the report.
  3. Observer bias: treatment knowledge influences outcome ratings.
  4. Interviewer bias: exposure questioning differs between groups.
  5. Hawthorne effect: observation changes actual behavior. An accurate measurement of that changed behavior is not itself an information error.

Review: Sample Size and Bias

  • Random error (sampling variability): Results vary by chance. More observations generally improve precision.
  • Bias: A systematic problem in selection or measurement can remain even in a very large study.

Example: Interviewing more people at local gyms does not fix the problem of estimating community activity from a sample that overrepresents gym users.

For bias, improve who is included or how measurements are made. Increasing the sample size alone does not repair those processes.

  • This example concerns estimating community activity, not every possible comparison among gym users.

  • A large biased sample can give a very precise answer to the wrong population question.

  • Then transition: even with careful recruitment and accurate measurement, groups can differ in other causes of the outcome.

Review: Confounding

Confounding: Other causes of the outcome get mixed into our estimate of the exposure’s effect. We may give credit or blame to the wrong variable.

Would reducing ice cream sales reduce drownings? This pattern alone does not show that it would.

  • The arrows express the simplified causal assumptions, not estimates from data.

  • Warm weather can raise sales and increase opportunities for swimming. The observed association may be useful for prediction without being the causal effect of ice cream sales.

Practice: Does the Class Increase Falls?

Hypothetical study: Follow 200 people for one year after some choose to attend a fall-prevention class.

People With at Least One Fall People Followed One-Year Risk
Attended class 28 100 28%
Did not attend 12 100 12%

Which conclusion is justified?

A. The class caused more falls.
B. Attendees had more falls, but they may have differed before class.
C. The class prevented falls.

Answer: B. These are invented counts for teaching, not findings about an actual program. Assume complete follow-up and accurate measurement so we can focus on confounding. The outcome is whether each person had at least one fall during the same one-year period, not the total number of falls.

Ask what might lead someone to join: a prior fall may both encourage attendance and predict future falls. Participants chose whether to attend; they were not randomized.

Comparing People With the Same Fall History

The same 200 people, now separated by whether they had fallen before the class began:

No Prior Fall Prior Fall All Participants
Attended class 1 / 10 = 10% 27 / 90 = 30% 28 / 100 = 28%
Did not attend 9 / 90 = 10% 3 / 10 = 30% 12 / 100 = 12%
  • Within each history group, the risks are the same.
  • Attendees included more people with prior falls: 90% versus 10%.

Accounting for fall history removes the apparent difference in this example.

  • Start with the rightmost column, “All Participants.” These are the same totals we just saw: 28 of 100 class attendees and 12 of 100 nonattendees had at least one fall during follow-up.

  • The rows still separate people by whether they attended the class, just as in the previous table. The “No Prior Fall” and “Prior Fall” columns now divide each attendance group by whether people had fallen before the class began.

  • In each cell, the first number is the number of people who had at least one fall during the following year. The second number is the total number of people in that particular attendance and fall-history group.

  • For example, read the first cell under “No Prior Fall” as: “Of the 10 class attendees with no prior fall, 1 fell during follow-up, a risk of 10%.” Then move down that column: among nonattendees with no prior fall, 9 of 90 fell, also 10%.

  • Now read down the “Prior Fall” column: 27 of 90 attendees and 3 of 10 nonattendees fell during follow-up. Both risks are 30%. Reading down each fall-history column lets us compare attendance groups with the same prior fall history.

  • This is stratification: compare people within categories of a relevant variable.

  • Students should interpret the supplied percentages; calculation or a pooled adjustment formula is not required.

  • All people and falls remain in the table. We changed the comparison, not the observations.

  • In this hypothetical story, previous falls influence attendance and predict subsequent falls.

  • Those risks became mixed into the overall comparison. If both groups had an equal mix of the two history categories, each would have a 20% risk: the average of 10% and 30%. This optional illustration is standardization, not an additional result students must calculate.

  • Equal observed risks do not prove that a real intervention has no effect, nor does any change after adjustment prove confounding. Here the constructed numbers and stated causal assumptions illustrate the mechanism. Sources for stratification and interpretation: Dicker (2025).

Prior Falls and Class Attendance

In our hypothetical study, prior falls can influence both class attendance and the chance of falling again.

In this hypothetical example, assume the class has no effect on falls.

The groups’ different fall histories explain the overall difference in risk.

  • Read each arrow in ordinary language. In this hypothetical story, having fallen before can motivate someone to attend the class and can increase their chance of falling again. Each arrow represents a proposed causal influence.

  • There is no arrow from class attendance to falls in the next year because we assume the class has no causal effect in this hypothetical example. This is an assumption of the teaching story, not a conclusion established by the table alone.

  • Connect the diagram to the tables: 90% of attendees had a prior fall, compared with 10% of nonattendees. Their different fall histories produced the overall risk difference, which we could mistakenly attribute to class attendance.

  • Comparing attendees and nonattendees within the same fall-history category gave equal risks in our constructed example. The diagram explains why we considered fall history. The numbers alone do not establish the arrows or prove that a real class has no effect.

  • Ask students to explain why prior falls matter for both attendance and future falls. Formal diagram terminology and path rules are not needed here. General diagram principles: Hernán et al. (2002).

Practice: Which Variable Needs Attention?

A study compares people who choose a fall-prevention class with people who do not.

Researchers learn that prior falls influence class attendance and the chance of another fall.

What is the best reason to consider prior falls when making the comparison?

A. We should control for every variable available.

B. Prior falls are a plausible common cause of attendance and later falls.

C. Any variable associated with the outcome must be controlled.

Answer: B. The stated causal knowledge identifies the reason for concern. Associations alone do not establish a variable’s role. A and C overlook mediators, colliders, and the question being asked. Use a show of hands or letter cards as a low-tech fallback.

This replaces the abstract inherited true/false questions with a question about the worked example. Students should explain the common cause, not select a statistical model.

Addressing Confounding During Study Design

Approach Basic Idea Example
Randomization Assign the intervention by chance Randomly offer the class
Restriction Limit a relevant characteristic Enroll only people with no prior falls
Matching Select comparison participants with similar characteristics Match participants on fall history

Randomization balances baseline factors on average, including factors we did not measure.

Restriction narrows the population studied. Matching still requires an appropriate analysis.

This is a conceptual menu, not instruction in conducting a matched analysis. Random assignment does not guarantee identical groups in a particular sample, and it does not solve every problem arising after assignment. Restriction or matching only addresses the relevant factors selected and measured; neither automatically removes all confounding. Matching on the wrong variables can also introduce problems. Sources: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2024); Dicker (2025).

Addressing Confounding During Analysis

  • Stratification: Compare exposed and unexposed people within categories of a confounder, as in the fall-history table.

  • Statistical adjustment: Use methods such as regression or standardization to account for appropriate measured factors.

  • Both approaches depend on choosing relevant variables and measuring them adequately.

When reading a study, ask: What did the researchers account for, why did they choose those factors, and what important differences might remain?

Students need to recognize these approaches and explain their purpose; they do not need to fit a regression model or calculate an adjusted effect. Confounding can remain when important factors are missing or measured poorly. Methods also rely on design and modeling assumptions. These methods do not make an observational comparison equivalent to randomization merely because an estimate is labeled “adjusted.” Source: Dicker (2025).
