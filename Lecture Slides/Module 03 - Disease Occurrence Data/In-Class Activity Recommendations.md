# In-Class Activity Recommendations: Disease Occurrence Data

The central priorities are **choosing a data source that fits the question, understanding who and what its records represent, and limiting conclusions to what the data support**. These recommendations assume introductory undergraduate learners, pairs or existing project groups of 3–4, and one internet-connected device per group. The activities are options for the time after the opening reading-comprehension questions, not a plan to use all of them.

Reading anchors use PDF page numbers, with textbook pages where verified. Placement references use verified titles from the supplied QMD; rendered slide numbers were unavailable. Oral sharing is voluntary, and discussion polls use anonymous responses or unsigned paper slips.

## 1. Which Data Would We Buy? — Anonymous Discussion Quiz

**Concept and reading anchor:** Matching sources to questions; “Epidemiology in the Era of Big Data,” PDF pp. 3–5, and “U.S. Census Bureau,” pp. 8–10. Students weigh primary collection against secondary use and recognize that data collected outside healthcare can help explain health.

**Why include it:** Moves beyond naming sources to defending a practical choice. Addresses the assumptions that newly collected data are always better and that only medical records count as useful health evidence.

**Student-facing content:**

> **Fictional scenario:** Our county wants to understand barriers to staying safe during extreme heat. We can fund one initial approach:
>
> **A.** Interview residents about cooling access and transportation. We can ask exactly what we need, but recruitment takes time and some residents may not respond.
>
> **B.** Analyze existing emergency-department records. They show serious health events, but miss people who never reach an emergency department.
>
> **C.** Combine existing housing, tree-cover, and population data. They describe neighborhood conditions, but do not tell us who became ill.
>
> Which would we start with? Submit the letter and one reason.
>
> What is the strongest case for another option?

**How to run it and timing:** **7 minutes:** 1 minute to introduce the choice, 1 to respond anonymously, 2 for pair discussion, 2 to compare volunteered reasoning, and 1 for an optional revote and takeaway. Preparation: one poll with no designated correct answer.

**Instructor notes and debrief:** A fits questions about residents’ experiences; B fits serious illness and healthcare use; C fits environmental conditions and geographic targeting. Each becomes more or less useful as the question changes. Ask: “What would our chosen source leave unanswered?”

Add a brief clarification because the supplied excerpt does not explicitly teach this distinction: *Primary collection gathers new observations for the current study; secondary use analyzes existing data. Neither label guarantees quality.* Neighborhood data can be relevant to health without containing diagnoses.

**Takeaway:** Choose the source after clarifying the question.

**Presentation placement and fallback:** Open the sources section after the comprehension quiz; replace or develop **“Epidemiologic Data.”** Use unsigned slips with letter-plus-reason responses if polling fails.

## 2. Reports Doubled. Did Mistreatment Double? — DETECT Story and Short Exercise

**Concept and reading anchor:** Administrative records, changing detection, and incomplete coverage; PDF pp. 3–5 and 22–23. Students distinguish a change in recorded reports from a change in underlying occurrence.

**Why include it:** Makes the DETECT work a concrete example of how an intervention can change the data-generating process.

**Student-facing content:**

> **Documented starting point:** DETECT introduced a structured screening tool into medics’ work with older adults. The pilot found increased reporting of suspected elder abuse to Adult Protective Services. [DETECT pilot abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30614399/)
>
> **Simulated teaching data—not DETECT results:**
>
> | Equal-length period | Eligible EMS encounters | Encounters generating a report |
> |---|---:|---:|
> | Before structured screening | 1,000 | 20 |
> | After structured screening | 1,000 | 40 |
>
> A draft headline says: “Elder mistreatment doubled.”
>
> Write a more defensible headline. Then name one additional piece of evidence that would help us distinguish increased mistreatment from increased detection.

**How to run it and timing:** **8 minutes:** 1 minute for the story, 3 for pairs to calculate and rewrite, 1 to submit anonymously, and 3 to compare headlines and needed evidence. Preparation: display the table and provide paper.

**Instructor notes and debrief:** Reporting increased from **2% to 4% of eligible encounters**: a 2-percentage-point increase and a doubling. A defensible headline is: “The percentage of eligible EMS encounters generating a report doubled after structured screening.”

Possible explanations include better recognition, changed reporting practices, different patients, or changing occurrence. Ask: “Could this increase represent an improvement in practice?” Follow with: “Would APS confirmation settle the question?” The later DETECT evaluation explicitly cautions against treating APS investigation outcomes as a perfect measure of true mistreatment. [DETECT evaluation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7206507/)

**Takeaway:** Administrative records describe events filtered through service access, observation, documentation, and reporting.

**Presentation placement and fallback:** Place in administrative data, developing **“Factors Affecting Epidemiologic Data Quality.”** The printed story and table require no internet; collect unsigned headlines.

## 3. One Connected Health Record: What Access Should Come With It? — Brief Supplement and Discussion Quiz

**Concept and reading anchor:** Data linkage and privacy, PDF pp. 3–5. Students weigh useful access against disclosure risks. HIPAA and PHI are supplemental concepts not explained in the supplied excerpt.

**Why include it:** Gives the universal-records idea a bounded decision. A broad international debate would require substantially more background than the reading provides.

**Student-facing content:**

> **Three terms**
>
> - **PII:** Information that identifies someone or can help identify them when combined with other information.
> - **PHI:** Individually identifiable health information protected under HIPAA in covered healthcare contexts.
> - **HIPAA:** Sets rules for covered organizations’ use and disclosure of protected health information; it does not govern every piece of health-related information everywhere.
>
> **Fictional proposal:** A state creates a system connecting participating hospitals’ records. Researchers want to study repeat hospitalizations.
>
> Which research-access approach should it prioritize?
>
> **A.** Public summary tables: broad access, limited ability to follow individuals.
>
> **B.** Approved access to linked individual records in a secure setting: more analytical detail, more oversight and privacy responsibility.
>
> **C.** A separate consenting research cohort: participant choice, additional recruitment and possible selection bias.
>
> Choose an approach and a safeguard. What research question would make another approach preferable?

**How to run it and timing:** **7 minutes:** 1 minute for terminology, 1 for anonymous responses, 2 for pairs, and 3 for discussion. Preparation: one poll and the terminology slide.

**Instructor notes and debrief:** A supports broad descriptive questions; B supports following repeat encounters; C supports purpose-specific participation but may exclude important groups. These approaches can coexist. The poll concerns priorities, not a legal determination.

Correct the factual claim that deleting names automatically makes records de-identified: dates, locations, and other details can remain identifying. HIPAA recognizes specific de-identification methods. [HHS guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/de-identification/index.html)

**Takeaway:** Linkage, central storage, and access permissions are separate design decisions.

**Presentation placement and fallback:** Follow administrative data and linkage; connect to **“Factors Affecting Epidemiologic Data Quality.”** Use unsigned paper responses. Keep the scenario written rather than relying on a video.

## 4. Find the Denominator That Belongs to Our Numerator — Census Exercise

**Concept and reading anchor:** “U.S. Census Bureau,” PDF pp. 8–10. Students retrieve a population estimate and match its geography, population, and year to an event count.

**Why include it:** Turns “the Census supplies denominators” into a decision that changes a calculated rate.

**Student-facing content:**

> **Simulated numerator:** During 2025, 2,248 Tarrant County residents died from a fictional condition.
>
> Open [Census QuickFacts: Tarrant County](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/tarrantcountytexas).
>
> 1. Find the population estimate most appropriate for this numerator. Record its value and date.
> 2. Calculate deaths per 100,000 residents.
> 3. Explain why the 2020 Census count is less suitable.
>
> **Extension:** If the deaths were only among residents aged 65 or older, what would need to change?

**How to run it and timing:** **9 minutes:** 1 minute of instructions, 3 for retrieval, 2 for calculation, and 3 for comparison and the extension. Pairs need a browser and calculator. Preparation: bookmark the page and retain the two population figures below as a fallback.

**Instructor notes and debrief:** QuickFacts currently reports **2,248,466 residents on July 1, 2025**, and a **2020 Census count of 2,110,640**. [Census source](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/tarrantcountytexas)

The intended calculation is:

$$
\frac{2,248}{2,248,466} \times 100,000 \approx 100.0
$$

Using the 2020 count gives **106.5 per 100,000**. The difference arises entirely from denominator selection. For deaths among older residents, use the corresponding older population, with compatible geography and year.

Ask: “Would the same denominator work for deaths occurring in Tarrant County hospitals, including nonresidents?”

**Takeaway:** A plausible population number is not necessarily the correct denominator.

**Presentation placement and fallback:** Start the Census section, before **“Vital Registration System.”** Print the two Census values with their labels; students still choose, calculate, and explain.

## 5. Find a Statistic—and Prevent Its Misuse — Vital-Statistics Retrieval Exercise

**Concept and reading anchor:** Online retrieval, PDF pp. 6–7, and vital statistics, pp. 11–15 and 39. Students retrieve a finding with its year, measure, and interpretation.

**Why include it:** Implements the preference for students finding statistics while adding a reasoning task beyond reporting numbers.

**Student-facing content:**

> Open [CDC’s *Mortality in the United States, 2024*](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db548.htm). Each pair takes one task:
>
> **A. Life expectancy:** Find life expectancy at birth. Explain why it is not a promise about how long a particular newborn will live.
>
> **B. Death rates:** Find the age-adjusted death rates for 2023 and 2024. Explain what age adjustment contributes to the comparison.
>
> **C. Leading causes:** Find which cause entered the top ten in 2024. Did its age-adjusted death rate increase?
>
> Produce two sentences: one accurate finding with year and units, and one interpretation or limitation.

**How to run it and timing:** **8 minutes:** 1 minute to assign tasks, 3 for retrieval, 1 to write, and 3 for up to three voluntary reports and synthesis. Preparation: link directly to the brief and have its tables available on paper.

**Instructor notes and debrief:** Verified answers are **79.0 years**; **750.5 versus 722.1 deaths per 100,000 standard population**; and **suicide**, whose age-adjusted rate declined from **14.1 to 13.7** even as it became the tenth leading cause. [CDC brief](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db548.htm)

Life expectancy summarizes mortality conditions; age adjustment facilitates comparisons accounting for age composition. Ranking is relative to other causes, so a rank can rise while a rate falls. Ask: “Which finding would we use to compare mortality across populations with different age structures?”

**Takeaway:** Report the measure and its meaning together.

**Presentation placement and fallback:** Replace the existing mortality-summary bullets and develop **“Leading Cause ≠ Highest Risk.”** Use printed data tables with the same tasks.

## 6. One Death, Several Conditions — Death-Certificate Interpretation Exercise

**Concept and reading anchor:** “The Vital Registration System and Vital Events,” PDF pp. 11–14, especially textbook p. 79. Students distinguish immediate, underlying, and contributing causes.

**Why include it:** Directly addresses the COVID-era misunderstanding that listing another condition necessarily invalidates COVID-19’s role in a death.

**Student-facing content:**

> **Fictional teaching case:** The certifying clinician documents this sequence:
>
> COVID-19 → pneumonia → acute respiratory distress syndrome → death.
>
> The clinician also documents diabetes as contributing to the death.
>
> A reader says: “The person died of respiratory distress, so this should not count as a COVID-19 death.”
>
> In two sentences, explain what the reader has misunderstood. Then identify one uncertainty that could still affect the accuracy of the death certificate.

**How to run it and timing:** **8 minutes:** 1 minute to explain the documented sequence, 2 for individual writing, 2 for pairs, and 3 for debrief. Introduce “underlying” as the condition initiating the sequence; students need not diagnose the case.

**Instructor notes and debrief:** In this supplied sequence, COVID-19 is the underlying cause, respiratory distress syndrome the immediate cause, and diabetes an additional contributing condition. Multiple listed conditions do not negate the underlying cause. CDC’s certification guidance explicitly illustrates reporting COVID-19 together with pneumonia and respiratory distress. [CDC guidance](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/86558)

Legitimate uncertainties include incomplete clinical information and errors in documenting the sequence. The factual correction is not an invitation to assume every certificate is accurate.

Ask: “How would ‘deaths with COVID-19 listed anywhere’ differ from ‘deaths with COVID-19 as the underlying cause’?”

**Takeaway:** Complete death registration and accurate cause classification are different qualities.

**Presentation placement and fallback:** Follow **“Vital Registration System”** or **“Vital Statistics Example: Texas Death Data.”** The written scenario is the low-tech version; collect unsigned responses if displaying examples.

## 7. Which Source Could Answer Our Project Question? — Survey Selection Exercise

**Concept and reading anchor:** BRFSS, PDF pp. 25–28; NCHS surveys and Table 4.6, pp. 33 and 39. Students select a source based on measurement and population coverage.

**Why include it:** Connects the module to the group project without repeating “NHANES has examinations.” It also prevents treating all federally supported datasets as interchangeable.

**Student-facing content:**

> **Source cards**
>
> | Source | What it offers |
> |---|---|
> | NHIS | Interview information about health conditions, functioning, behaviors, and healthcare access. |
> | NHANES | Interviews plus examinations and laboratory measurements; useful for conditions that may be undiagnosed. |
> | BRFSS | State-level adult survey information about behaviors, chronic conditions, and preventive services. |
> | All of Us | A research cohort combining several kinds of participant data; not a representative sample of the United States. |
>
> For our disease topic—or diabetes if we have not chosen one—write:
>
> “To learn ___ about ___, we would first examine ___ because ___. We would still need to check ___.”
>
> Name one measurement we would seek and one competing source that would answer a different question.
>
> Our existing project still uses CDI or an approved alternative; this exercise does not change that requirement.

**How to run it and timing:** **10 minutes:** 1 minute for instructions, 2 to read the cards, 3 for groups to formulate a question, 1 to check a linked source description, and 3 to compare two recommendations. Preparation: provide the cards and links; no raw-data analysis or account creation.

**Instructor notes and debrief:** For diabetes, NHIS can support questions about reported diagnosis and healthcare access; NHANES adds relevant measured information; BRFSS suits many state comparisons. Students must check the actual year, items, eligible population, and available geography. [NHIS](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhis/about/index.html), [NHANES](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about/index.html), [BRFSS](https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/index.html)

**Supplement:** All of Us is absent from the supplied reading and should be introduced as a research cohort, not another population survey. Its convenience sample does not automatically support national prevalence estimates. [All of Us sampling guidance](https://www.researchallofus.org/faq/will-the-all-of-us-cohort-offer-a-representative-sample-of-u-s-citizens/)

Ask: “Would interviewing twice as many people solve the problem if the measurement we need is absent?”

**Takeaway:** Source suitability depends on measurement and sampling, not just size.

**Presentation placement and fallback:** Develop **“National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS)”** into a broader sources section; BRFSS and All of Us should not be labeled NCHS programs. Printed cards fully support the exercise.

## 8. A Parkinson’s Registry Grows. What Grew? — Anonymous Discussion Quiz

**Concept and reading anchor:** “Case Registries,” PDF pp. 29–32. Students distinguish registry enrollment from population incidence or prevalence.

**Why include it:** Uses the TCU registry context without assuming unprovided details about its design.

**Student-facing content:**

> **Hypothetical scenario inspired by the registry you described—not actual TCU registry data:** A Parkinson’s registry adds two participating clinics and an online enrollment option. Enrollment rises from 200 to 350 people.
>
> We can fund one improvement next:
>
> **A.** Recruit from more locations, to reach people outside the current clinics.
>
> **B.** Confirm eligibility and remove duplicate records, to improve confidence in who is counted.
>
> **C.** Improve follow-up, to understand changes among people already enrolled.
>
> Which should come first, and what should the registry’s main purpose be for that choice to make sense?
>
> What is the strongest case for another option?

**How to run it and timing:** **7 minutes:** 1 minute to introduce the scenario, 1 for anonymous voting, 2 for pairs, and 3 for debrief. Preparation: one poll.

**Instructor notes and debrief:** A fits broader coverage, B fits case-count accuracy, and C fits longitudinal questions. Priorities depend on purpose and current weaknesses.

The increase does not establish increased disease occurrence: expanded recruitment alone could produce it. A registry intended to estimate population incidence needs defined geography, eligibility, diagnosis timing, and adequate case ascertainment. Ask: “What would justify calling a newly enrolled person a newly diagnosed case?”

**Takeaway:** A registry’s usefulness depends on its design; its name does not guarantee complete population coverage.

**Presentation placement and fallback:** Place after surveys, using the registry example within **“Public Health Surveillance Programs”** as the transition. Use unsigned slips.

## 9. What Does a Google Trends Peak Actually Tell Us? — Guided Exploration and Claim Revision

**Concept and reading anchor:** Nontraditional data, PDF pp. 3–5. Students distinguish search activity from disease occurrence and from the intentions behind searches.

**Why include it:** Offers a quick hands-on experience with data collected outside health monitoring while producing a concrete, defensible claim.

**Student-facing content:**

> Open [Google Trends](https://trends.google.com/trends/). Compare the **search terms** “elder abuse” and “elder mistreatment,” using:
>
> **United States · January 1–December 31, 2023 · Web Search**
>
> Record the settings and one pattern we observe.
>
> Write:
>
> - One sentence describing the search data.
> - One tempting conclusion these data cannot establish.
>
> Then answer: “What else could make searches increase besides an increase in mistreatment?”
>
> **Reading the scale:** Values represent relative search interest on a 0–100 scale, not numbers of people or cases. Low-volume searches can appear as zero. [Google’s explanation](https://support.google.com/trends/answer/4365533?hl=en)

**How to run it and timing:** **9 minutes:** 1 minute of setup, 3 to explore, 2 to write, and 3 to compare claims and alternative explanations. Preparation: pre-open the comparison and retain an accessible table or screenshot with a text description.

**Instructor notes and debrief:** Accept descriptions tied to the displayed settings; do not require a particular peak. Reject claims that zero means no mistreatment or that an index of 100 means 100 cases. Possible explanations for increased searching include news coverage, coursework, campaigns, caregivers seeking help, or changing terminology.

The 2026 study makes a particularly useful debrief: it documents differences in search interest and explicitly acknowledges that Google Trends is not a validated measure of public awareness. [Elder-mistreatment study](https://formative.jmir.org/2026/1/e83797)

The delta-8 paper provides an alternative example of using searches to investigate emerging interest, while leaving actual use prevalence unresolved. [Delta-8 study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35080939/)

**Takeaway:** State the observed signal before interpreting what it might represent.

**Presentation placement and fallback:** Place after registries, returning to the nontraditional sources introduced in **“Epidemiologic Data.”** If access fails, supply **simulated indices of 25, 50, and 100 across three weeks** and ask students to revise “Cases quadrupled.” Explain that the simulation illustrates interpretation, not findings.

## 10. Google Flu Trends: Retire It, Repair It, or Change Its Role? — Documented Story and Discussion Quiz

**Concept and reading anchor:** Google Flu Trends and big-data limitations, PDF pp. 4–5. Students distinguish prediction from explanation and decide what validation a useful signal requires.

**Why include it:** Extends the reading’s brief caution into a consequential decision, without requiring students to learn predictive modeling.

**Student-facing content:**

> **Documented story:** Google Flu Trends used search information to estimate influenza activity. In early 2013, its estimate exceeded twice the CDC estimate of the proportion of doctor visits for influenza-like illness—the measure it was intended to predict. [Lazer and colleagues](https://gking.harvard.edu/publication/the-parable-of-google-flu-traps-in-big-data-analysis/)
>
> After a failure like this, what role should the tool have?
>
> **A.** Suspend operational use until it meets an agreed accuracy standard.
>
> **B.** Keep it as an early signal that triggers checks against other sources.
>
> **C.** Rebuild it using search and conventional surveillance data, then evaluate it on later observations.
>
> Choose a course of action and name one condition that would change our judgment.

**How to run it and timing:** **7 minutes:** 1 minute for the story, 1 for anonymous voting, 2 for pairs to consider another option, and 3 for discussion and an optional revote. Preparation: written story and poll; no video needed.

**Instructor notes and debrief:** A prioritizes avoiding harmful decisions; B may suit inexpensive verification steps; C invests in improving the model. Choices depend on the costs of false alarms, missed events, and rebuilding.

The paper discusses changing search behavior and the platform’s changing algorithms as threats to prediction. Present these as mechanisms to examine, not a proven single explanation for every error. [Full paper](https://gking.harvard.edu/files/0314policyforumff.pdf)

Ask: “If prediction became excellent, would that show that searching causes influenza?” It would not. Also clarify that influenza-like illness is not equivalent to laboratory-confirmed influenza.

**Takeaway:** A predictive association can be useful without being causal, but usefulness still requires validation.

**Presentation placement and fallback:** Close the nontraditional-data section, developing **“Key Takeaways: Epidemiologic Data.”** Use the written story and unsigned ballots.

## Recommended Sequence

Use **1 → 2 → 7 → 9**, totaling **34 minutes**, excluding the opening quiz. This sequence moves from choosing a source, through understanding administrative records, to selecting a project-relevant source and interpreting unfamiliar data.

If mortality interpretation is the priority for this meeting, substitute **6 for 9**, bringing the sequence to **33 minutes**.
