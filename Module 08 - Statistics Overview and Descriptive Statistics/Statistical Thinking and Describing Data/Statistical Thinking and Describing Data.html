What conclusions can the group draw from these data?

Descriptive and Inferential Statistics

Descriptive Statistics

Describe the sample we observed.

Inferential Statistics

Use sample data to make inferences about the target population.

  • Connect to the preceding slide: we describe the 150 respondents, then ask what their data can tell us about all TCU students enrolled in Fall 2026.
  • Sampling error is chance variation: another random sample could have a different mean sleep duration even with sound sampling. It does not mean someone made a mistake.
  • Bias is systematic error. Recruiting only at one library, differences between respondents and nonrespondents, or inaccurate sleep reports could distort the estimate. A larger sample does not automatically remove these problems.
  • Confounding becomes relevant when we interpret the sleep–stress association, especially as a possible causal effect. For example, workload could influence both sleep and stress and help explain their association. Confounding is not the central concern when estimating mean sleep duration alone.
  • Inferential methods quantify uncertainty under assumptions about how the data were collected and analyzed. They do not automatically correct bias or confounding.

Question: Describing or Inferring?

Statement A

“The 150 respondents reported an average of 6.2 hours of sleep.”

Statement B

“We estimate the average sleep duration of all TCU students from the sample.”

  1. Which statement describes observed data? Which makes an inference?
  2. What would you need to know before trusting Statement B?
  • Give pairs 2 minutes, then collect two responses.
  • Statement A is descriptive: it summarizes the respondents. Statement B is inferential: it uses their data to estimate a characteristic of all TCU students.
  • Expected issues: recruitment, nonresponse, whether different student groups were represented, the measurement question, and the period measured.
  • Connect to the library example: students leaving one library on one evening may differ from the target population, and respondents may differ from nonrespondents.
  • Samples can vary even with a sound sampling process; a biased recruitment process is a separate concern.
  • Adapted from the think–pair–share activity on slide 9 of Yan Zhang’s Statistics Overview and Descriptive Statistics PowerPoint. The TCU setting and 6.2-hour mean are fictional adaptations; the source uses percentages of students reporting poor sleep.
  • Low-tech fallback: pair discussion and verbal responses.

Population and Sample Summaries

We use the mean sleep duration in our sample to estimate the mean in our target population.

What do we call a numerical summary of a population? Of a sample?

Target population Sample
Numerical summary Parameter Statistic
Example Mean sleep for all TCU students Mean sleep for 150 respondents
Mean symbol μ\mu x‾\bar{x}

A sample statistic can estimate a population parameter.

  • Connect to Statement B on the previous slide: the sample mean is known, and we use it to estimate the unknown mean for the target population.
  • Ask the class to recall both terms before revealing the table. Expected answers: population parameter and sample statistic.
  • After revealing the table, connect the terms to the mean symbols: mu for the population mean and x-bar for the sample mean.
  • Adapted from Yan slides 15-16.
  • Emphasize what group is summarized, rather than memorizing a Greek-versus-Roman rule.
  • Not all parameter symbols are Greek.
  • The population parameter is fixed for the defined population and measurement period; a statistic can vary from sample to sample.

Question: Statistic or Parameter?

The mean sleep duration among 150 surveyed TCU students is 6.2 hours. What is 6.2 hours?

A. A population parameter

B. A sample statistic

C. Both

D. Neither

  • Answer: B. The mean summarizes the 150 respondents.
  • The mean for all enrolled TCU students would be a population parameter.
  • A statistic may estimate a parameter, but the two need not be equal.
  • Allow 3 minutes, including discussion.
  • Have students write A, B, C, or D on paper, compare with a partner, then vote by raising fingers.
  • Discuss the reason before revealing the answer.

How would you define a variable? Give one example from a student health survey.

Review: Categorical Variables

What are the two types of categorical variables? How do they differ?

Kind Distinction Example
Nominal Categories without a natural order Major; yes/no response
Ordinal Categories with an order Low/moderate/high stress

A numerical code does not automatically make a category quantitative.

  • Ask students to call out the two types, explain the difference, and offer an example before revealing the table.
  • A binary variable has two categories.
  • For example, 0 = no and 1 = yes remains a categorical response.

Review: Quantitative Variables

What are the two types of quantitative variables? How do they differ?

Kind Distinction Example
Discrete quantitative Countable numerical values Number of drinks
Continuous quantitative Measurements along a continuum Sleep duration
  • Ask students to call out the two types, explain the difference, and offer an example before revealing the table.
  • Recorded sleep may be rounded to half-hours, but duration is the underlying continuous quantity.
  • Do not classify counts as continuous.
  • Interval and ratio describe another aspect of measurement: equal intervals and whether zero has a meaningful interpretation.
  • If asked, temperature in Celsius is interval, while elapsed duration has a meaningful zero.
  • Keep this distinction brief rather than adding another taxonomy lesson.

Classify the Recorded Values

Classify each variable.

  1. Caffeine use: yes / no
  2. Sleep duration: 5.5 / 6.0 / 7.5 hours
  3. Stress rating: low / moderate / high
  4. Number of nights studied past midnight: 0 / 1 / 2 / … / 7
  • Allow about 3 minutes for whole-class discussion. Ask for a classification and a brief explanation for each variable.
  • Answers: nominal binary, continuous quantitative, ordinal, discrete quantitative.
  • The wording and recorded values determine the classification.
  • A numeric stress score is not the same variable as a low/moderate/high stress category; use the project’s codebook when interpreting the actual score.
  • If responses differ, ask students to explain which recorded values support their classification before confirming the answer.

What Grouping Hides

The 7 to less than 8 hours interval contains 3 students.

From that grouped table alone:

  • We know there are three observations in the interval.
  • We do not know how many are exactly 7 hours.
  • We cannot recover the exact mean sleep duration.
  • Ask for answers before revealing the explanation verbally.
  • Zero, one, two, or three people could be exactly 7 based only on the grouped table.
  • We happen to know there are two from the original data, but that information was lost when we grouped the values.
  • This retains Yan’s grouped-frequency interpretation exercise and prepares students for the grouped-table question in Quiz 4 without copying its test wording.

Question: Which Denominator?

Of 12 fictional students, 6 report less than 7 hours of sleep. Of those 6, 4 report high stress. What percentage of the shorter-sleep group reports high stress?

A. 4 / 12 = 33.3%

B. 6 / 12 = 50.0%

C. 4 / 6 = 66.7%

D. 6 / 4 = 150.0%

  • Answer: C. The question restricts the denominator to the 6 students in the shorter-sleep group.
  • Four of those 6 report high stress.
  • The fraction 4/12 answers a different question: what proportion of the whole sample is in both categories?
  • Allow 4 minutes, including discussion.
  • Have students write A, B, C, or D on paper, compare with a partner, then vote by raising fingers.
  • Discuss the reason before revealing the answer.

How Would We Summarize Sleep Hours?

We have described categories using counts and percentages.

What should we ask about the continuous sleep measurements to summarize them?

  • What is a typical value? Center
  • How different are the values? Spread
  • What does the distribution look like? Shape
  • Ask the class for the questions we should ask about continuous data before revealing the bullets. Accept everyday language, then connect their responses to center, spread, and shape.
  • Use the final 2 minutes of the group block to connect its categorical summaries to continuous summaries.
  • This is only an introduction to center and spread; the next meeting develops these concepts and their spreadsheet procedures.

Review: Mean, Median, and Mode

Sorted sleep hours:

5, 5.5, 6, 6, 6.5, 6.5, 7, 7, 7.5, 8, 8, 9

Calculate the mean, median, and mode.

Mean: total hours ÷ number of students = 6.83 hours

Median: average of the two middle values = 6.75 hours

Modes: 6, 6.5, 7, and 8 hours — each appears twice.

  • Center/spread block: 8 minutes through “The Same Mean Can Hide Different Spread.”
  • Give students about 2 minutes to calculate all three summaries; calculators are welcome. Ask for their answers and reasoning before revealing the solutions.
  • Have students locate the middle positions before discussing the answer: the 6th and 7th values are 6.5 and 7, giving 6.75.
  • The total is 82 hours, so 82/12 = 6.8333.
  • Do not require a long hand-calculation exercise.
  • The mode is the most frequent value or category; this dataset has several tied modal values (6, 6.5, 7, and 8).
  • Its tied modes are a useful reason not to force a single mode or infer skew from a mean/median/mode ordering.

The Same Mean Can Hide Different Spread

Fictional group Sleep hours Mean Range
A 6, 7, 8 7
B 4, 7, 10 7

Calculate the range for each group. Which group has more spread?

  • Ask students to calculate both ranges and explain which group varies more before revealing the table.
  • Group A: 8 − 6 = 2 hours. Group B: 10 − 4 = 6 hours.
  • Both means are 7, but group B varies more.
  • Range is maximum minus minimum.
  • Introduce SD as a measure of spread around the mean and IQR as the spread of the middle half of values; defer calculation and detailed interpretation to Wednesday.
  • Ask students to explain the difference in words.
  • This is a small conceptual check with a verbal-response fallback.
  • Do not infer distribution shape from only three observations.
